There is a very specific feeling of a road trip going wrong: it's 4pm, you're 90 minutes behind, the thing you drove three hours for closes at 5, and the person in the passenger seat is looking at you like it's your fault. It usually *is* nobody's fault. The plan was just built in the wrong order.

Here's the order we've settled on after a lot of miles. It doesn't matter if you use a notebook, a spreadsheet, or [an app that does most of it for you](/how-wanderply-plans-your-trip) — the sequence is the thing.

## Step 1 — Fix the constraints before the destinations

Before you list a single stop, write down what you *can't* change:

- **Dates** — and which ones are hard (a flight home) versus soft (a hotel you haven't booked).
- **Who's coming** — ages, mobility, how long they'll tolerate the car per day. Kids and grandparents both cap a driving day at ~5 hours.
- **The car** — yours, a rental, or are you flying in? This changes *everything* about the route.
- **The budget** — a number, not a vibe. It decides "hotel or campground" and "national park lodge or the town outside."

Everything downstream is a function of these. Skipping this step is why plans need rebuilding on day two.

## Step 2 — Pick the anchors (two to four, not twenty)

Anchors are the *reasons* for the trip: the sunset at Delicate Arch, your friend's wedding, the one hike, the game. Two to four for a week. Not more.

Write each with its **duration and timing constraint** — "Delicate Arch — sunset, ~3 hrs including the hike," "Disneyland — 2 full days." Everything else gets scheduled *around* these; they don't move.

## Step 3 — Draw the shape of the route

Now — only now — open the map. Put the anchors and your start/end on it and find the shape: a **loop** (best — no doubling back), an **out-and-back** (fine — different stops each way), or a **point-to-point** (flying home from somewhere else).

Then break it into **driving days**, each under your group's tolerance from Step 1. This tells you where you'll *sleep*, which is the single most useful output of the whole exercise. Book those first — lodging near national parks and small towns is the thing that sells out.

## Step 4 — Fill the drive, not just the destinations

The mistake most planners make: they treat the drive as dead time between the real stops. But a 5-hour drive with two good 30-minute stops is *better* than a 4-hour drive with none — the stops become the memories, and everyone arrives less wrecked.

For each driving day, find one or two things **within 15 minutes of the road**: a viewpoint, a diner everyone talks about, a state park with a 20-minute walk, a weird roadside museum. Give them a *time* in the plan, not just a pin. ## Step 5 — Set a pace, then subtract 20%

Decide the *pace* honestly: relaxed, balanced, or packed. Then take whatever you scheduled and remove a fifth of it. Every road trip runs behind. Traffic, a bison jam, a longer lunch, a stop you loved and didn't want to leave. A plan with slack survives; a plan that's exactly full breaks the first time reality happens.

Build one **empty afternoon** into any trip over four days. You'll fill it, but you'll fill it with what you discovered, which is the best kind of stop.

## Step 6 — Weather and reservations, together

Look at the *typical* weather for your dates (not a 10-day forecast you can't see yet): highs, lows, monsoon season, snow closures. It changes what to pack and which day to hike. Then lock the reservations that gate the plan: **timed-entry parks** (Arches, Glacier, Rocky Mountain, Yosemite in peak season), **permit lotteries** (Angels Landing, Half Dome), and any restaurant that's a genuine anchor.

## Step 7 — Make it survive the drive

The final step is the one people skip: **get the plan off the internet.** Signal disappears exactly where road trips get interesting. Whatever you built has to open in airplane mode — the route, the addresses, the reservation numbers, the packing list. Print a one-pager as backup. Download offline maps for the whole region.

And decide who reads the plan aloud. Someone in the car has to be the navigator, and it can't be the driver.
